Vim's editing grammar

Vim commands are a verb and a noun, composed at the moment you type them. Most of the commands you will use were never designed by anyone.

Learning vim as a list of shortcuts is learning the wrong thing, and it is why the list feels endless. There are about eight verbs and thirty nouns. Everything else is the two multiplied together, which means the useful question is never "what is the shortcut for this" but "what is the verb, and what is the noun".

Pick a verb and a noun. Then move the cursor: click any character in the buffer, or focus it and use h j k l. The noun decides the region, the verb decides what happens to it.

The trade that buys the vocabulary

A keyboard has roughly fifty keys you can reach without a modifier. In an ordinary editor every one of them is spoken for: press d, you get a d. Commands therefore have to live somewhere worse, behind a chord or a menu or the mouse.

Vi's move was to notice that most editor time is not spent composing new text. It is spent moving through and restructuring text that already exists. So it gave that activity the whole keyboard, and made typing the state you enter deliberately and leave promptly. Open a file and d means delete, w means word, p means paste. The letters have become a vocabulary.

The cost is real: you have to know which state you are in, and every horror story about vim is that cost being paid before the benefit arrives. The benefit is not saved keystrokes. It is that single-letter commands can be placed next to each other and mean something.

Verb, then noun

The rule is one line:

{operator}{noun} — the operator acts on exactly the text the noun describes.

Nobody implemented dap. It exists because d exists and ap exists. That is why learning one more noun is worth more than learning one more command: a noun arrives already multiplied by every verb you have.

Doubling the operator means the current line, whole lines only: dd, yy, cc, >>. For two-letter operators the last letter doubles, so guu lowercases a line.

Counts sit in front of either half and multiply if you use both, so 2d3w deletes six words. In practice 3dd is the only one worth reaching for. A noun that names its target beats a count that arrives at it, because counting rows on screen is something humans are slow and bad at.

Two kinds of noun

This is the distinction that takes longest to see and explains the most once seen.

A motion describes travel. It begins at the cursor and goes somewhere: w, $, }, f(. What it covers therefore depends entirely on where the cursor happens to be.

A text object describes a region. It does not start at the cursor, it contains the cursor: iw, i(, ap. Anywhere inside produces the same result.

Put the cursor in the middle of a word in the panel and compare dw with diw. The motion deletes the tail of the word. The object deletes the word. Now move the cursor two characters and try both again. Only one of them changed its mind.

Objects come in i (inner) and a (around) forms, and the two families define that split differently:

  • Pair objects — i(, a", i{, at. Delimited. i is the contents, a adds the delimiters.
  • Shape objects — iw, ap, is. No delimiters, only whitespace. i is the thing, a adds the whitespace that separates it from its neighbours.

That second one is why daw closes the gap and diw leaves a double space, and why dap leaves a file correctly spaced where dip leaves a doubled blank line.

The two families also behave differently when the cursor is outside the region, which is a genuine inconsistency rather than something you have misunderstood. Quote objects search forward on the line, so ci" works from the start of the line. Bracket objects do not search at all, so ci( from outside the parens simply beeps. Try i( in the panel with the cursor on return and then on path.

Why the noun list is open-ended

When you press d, vim deletes nothing. It enters a state that is waiting for a noun, and only then acts. That state is a real mode called operator-pending, not a metaphor for one: neovim will show the half-typed command in the corner while you sit in it, and Esc gets you out.

Because it is a real mode, anything that can answer the question "which region" works with every operator automatically. Nobody writes the combination daf. You install one plugin that defines af as "a function", and daf, yaf, caf, >af, gcaf and !af all exist that afternoon.

This matters because vim's built-in nouns know about characters, whitespace and matched delimiters, and nothing about your language. There is no built-in object for a function, an argument, or a Python block, since indentation has no closing brace for i{ to find. That gap is filled by treesitter-based objects in neovim, and it is the strongest argument for neovim over vim on editing alone.

The two rules that make it exact

The grammar looks arbitrary at first because dw, de and dj all behave differently. They follow two properties that every noun carries.

Linewise or characterwise

A linewise noun operates on whole lines and discards your column. The vertical motions are linewise — j, k, G, gg, H, L — which is why dj removes two entire lines and cannot do anything else. Everything horizontal is characterwise. This is also why dd works at all: a doubled operator is a linewise noun covering the current line.

Inclusive or exclusive

A characterwise noun either takes the character it lands on or stops before it.

Exclusive, stops beforeInclusive, takes it
w b 0 ^ { } /e ge f t $ %

So de ends on the last letter of the word and dw runs to the start of the next one, taking the space between. And df( swallows the paren where dt( leaves it, which is the entire reason t exists.

You do not need to memorise the table. :help w prints the word exclusive in the entry and :help e prints inclusive. When a delete takes one character too many, that is the thing to look up.

Two deliberate exceptions. cw behaves like ce when the cursor is on a word, because changing a word and then having to retype the space it ate would be useless. And dw on the last word of a line stops at the line end instead of joining the next line. Both are documented, both break the rule on purpose. Knowing they exist stops you concluding you have misread the grammar.

Repeatability changes which command is right

. repeats the last change, not the last command. Motions do not count as changes and do not reset it, so you can travel as far as you like in between. An entire insert session, from i to Esc, is one change: type A then a comma then Esc, and j. on the next line appends a comma there too, correctly, on a line of a different length.

This retroactively grades commands you already know. ciw beats cw not for the keystroke but because it works from anywhere in the word, so the repeat does too:

/old_name → ciw new_name → Esc → n . n .

That is find-and-replace with a human confirming each site, assembled from three things you already have.

It is also the concrete reason to prefer a noun over a visual selection. . does work after a visual-mode operation, but it repeats the dimensions: the same number of lines, or the same lines and columns for a block. So Vjjjjd then . deletes five lines wherever you are next, while dap then . deletes the next paragraph, whatever length it happens to be. One repeats a shape, the other repeats an intention.

Undo uses the same unit from the other side. One u undoes one change, which is why typing four lines and pressing u removes all four. Small, self-contained edits repeat cleanly and undo cleanly; a fifteen-keystroke wander in and out of insert mode does neither.

There is no clipboard

There are about thirty-six named slots called registers, and every yank, delete and change writes to at least one. :reg prints them all.

Register " is the default, and it receives yanks and deletes alike, which produces the single most common complaint about vim. Copy a line with yy, delete the line you meant to replace with dd, press p, and you get back the line you just deleted. Nothing is broken. Cut and copy are the same slot, and cut happens more often than you notice.

Register 0 receives yanks and nothing else. Deletes never touch it. So "0p means "paste the thing I actually copied", and it is the highest-value two characters in this whole page. The other direction works too: "_dd deletes into the black hole register and leaves your yank alone.

Two more worth having early. "+ is the system clipboard, so "+yap copies a function out to paste in Slack. And Ctrl-r 0 pastes a register without leaving insert mode, which on the command line has the variant Ctrl-r Ctrl-w to insert the word under the cursor.

What people get wrong

That vim is fast because it saves keystrokes. Keystroke counting is the weakest case for it and loses to a good IDE on plenty of tasks. The case is composition and repeatability: nouns multiply across verbs, and . replays an intention rather than a shape.

That g is an operator. Reasonable guess, since gu and gU take a noun exactly as d and c do. It is not one. g is a namespace prefix, like z, [, ] and Ctrl-w: vim ran out of keys and opened a second page of the keyboard. What sits on that page is unrelated — gu and gq are operators, gg and gj are motions, and gv, gi, gf are neither. :help g is the index.

That i means insert. It means insert in normal mode. After an operator it means "inner" and waits for an object. A key has no global meaning in vim; the whole keyboard is reinterpreted per mode, which is exactly what buys the vocabulary. i I a A o O are not a special category either — they are ordinary normal-mode commands that happen to change mode, sitting in the same bucket as p, x and u.

That hjkl proves the design is a relic. Two things get conflated here. The key placement is an accident: Bill Joy wrote vi in 1976 on an ADM-3A terminal with arrows printed on those four keycaps and Escape where Tab is now. Modal editing is not an accident. The terminal died and the idea kept earning its keep, which is why it has since been reimplemented in VS Code, JetBrains, Emacs, browsers and shells.

Vim or neovim

For everything above, there is no difference. Same modes, same motions, same operators, same registers, and a vim tutorial from 2005 is still accurate about all of it.

What actually differs: neovim's config is Lua and its defaults are sane out of the box, where a bare vim .vimrc spends its first lines undoing 1976. Neovim ships an LSP client, treesitter, and a comment operator gc that vim needs a plugin for. Neovim also maps Y to y$ by default, finally making it consistent with D and C — though some distributions map it back, so check with :verbose nmap Y rather than trusting it.

Version-dependent, so worth checking rather than assuming. Pasting over a visual selection with P instead of p leaves the register untouched, so the paste can be repeated. That behaviour arrived in vim 8.2.4242 and is in current neovim, but older builds and vim emulation layers in other editors may not have it. Neovim's clipboard also depends on an external tool (xclip, wl-copy, win32yank); if "+y silently does nothing, :checkhealth clipboard says why. Vim fails differently here — clipboard support is compiled in or it is not, and :echo has('clipboard') returning 0 means no amount of installing tools will help.

Recall

Why does dw take the trailing space when cw does not?

w is exclusive and lands on the start of the next word, so the region runs from the cursor up to but not including that character, which means the space in between goes.

cw should behave identically, and does not. It is a deliberate exception: it acts like ce when the cursor is on a word, because you are about to type a replacement and would otherwise have to retype the space you just ate. dw keeps the original behaviour because when you delete a word you usually do want the gap to close.

You delete a five-line function with V4jd. The next function is seven lines. Why does . fail, and what should you have typed?

. after a visual-mode operation repeats the size, not the meaning: the same number of lines, or the same lines and columns for a block. So it deletes five lines of a seven-line function.

dap instead. The noun names a region rather than measuring one, so the repeat re-evaluates it at the new cursor position and takes the whole paragraph, whatever its length. This is the general reason to prefer an operator plus noun over a selection whenever a noun exists for what you mean.

You yy a line, navigate, dd the line you meant to replace, then p. The wrong text appears. What happened, and name both fixes.

Yanks and deletes both write to the unnamed register, and p reads from it, so the dd overwrote the yy.

Fix one, after the fact: "0p. Register 0 only ever receives yanks, so it still holds the line. Fix two, in advance: "_dd throws the deleted line into the black hole register and leaves the unnamed one intact. "0p gets used more, because you usually notice the problem after the delete rather than before it.

Nobody at any point wrote code implementing the command >ip. Explain why it works.

Pressing > puts vim into operator-pending mode, a real mode whose only job is to wait for something that answers "which region". ip answers it. The operator then applies to whatever came back.

Since the two halves are independent, every operator works with every noun without anyone enumerating the pairs. It is also why a plugin that adds one new object immediately gains every operator you know, and why vim's plugin ecosystem composes better than most editors' do.

i appears twice in vim's command tables with unrelated meanings. Why is that not a conflict?

Because the meaning is resolved by mode, not by key. In normal mode i enters insert mode. In operator-pending or visual mode it means "inner" and waits for an object. Nothing is shared between the two.

That reinterpretation is the whole trade: giving up the guarantee that a key does one fixed thing is what makes fifty keys carry hundreds of meanings. It is also why hitting Esc when you are confused is the right reflex — it puts you in a known state, so you know which reading the next key will get.
